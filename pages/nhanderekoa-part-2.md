              content: |-
                Between the 19th and early 20th centuries, large Guarani migrations occurred from the border region of Paraná and Mato Grosso do Sul to the state of São Paulo.

                As identified by Curt Nimuendajú, in the 1810s, 1820s, and 1870s, and possibly during other periods of the 19th century, migrations of Guarani groups from the Iguatemi region, on the border between Paraná and Mato Grosso, were recorded. In 1910, another large Guarani group is known to have migrated from the Brazil-Paraguay-Argentina border region to the Itariri region in the Ribeira Valley (Vale do Ribeira), today the Itariri Indigenous Territory. Between 1923 and 1946, successive migrations of Guarani groups from southern Brazil to the state of São Paulo were recorded, settling mainly along the coast.

                These latest groups form the generation immediately preceding many of today’s residents of the Jaraguá and Tenondé Porã Indigenous Territories in the city of São Paulo. Among them is Indian Chief Jandira, founder of Tekoa Ytu in the early 1960s, the first tekoa of the territorial recovery of Jaraguá. 

                After the creation of the Indian Protection Service (SPI) in 1910, Guarani peoples became the target of a systematic policy of forced settlement. In the state of São Paulo, the SPI sought to concentrate Guarani families at the Bananal Indigenous Post in Itanhaém, on the coast, and at the Araribá Indigenous Post, inland, emptying the city of São Paulo of Guarani presence.  

                Through their ancestral resistance, fleeing the forced settlements, some families began territorial recoveries in the city of São Paulo, in Jaraguá and in the Parelheiros region, today the Jaraguá and Tenondé Porã Indigenous Territories.

                Following the developmentalist logic of the 1960s and 1970s, the military dictatorship period was marked by investment in mega-infrastructure and authoritarian planning. 

                In the city of São Paulo, large urban and highway works were carried out, such as the Bandeirantes Highway, which directly struck the ancestral Guarani territory of Jaraguá. Pico do Jaraguá was designated to host communication antenna towers, and land was granted to Grupo Bandeirantes, TV Globo, the Padre Anchieta Foundation (TV Cultura) and the Army. In 1972, a large tourist complex was built in the park.

                In the wake of these projects, real-estate speculation and urbanization expanded dramatically along the edges of São Paulo, advancing over the forests of Jaraguá. The decades between 1960 and 1980 saw high rates of deforestation on the northern fringe of the city, with the densification of neighborhoods such as Jaraguá and Pirituba.

                As the military dictatorship’s “great development projects” advanced across Brazil, Indigenous peoples were repressed by the regime and their rights systematically violated. 

                As shown in the report of the [National Truth Commission](https://cnv.memoriasreveladas.gov.br/images/pdf/relatorio/Volume%202%20-%20Texto%205.pdf), within the context of national development plans, numerous Indigenous communities were subjected to forced transfers and removals carried out by state agents to clear land for infrastructure projects.

                This is the case of the construction of the Itaipu Hydroelectric Plant at the center of the ancestral Guarani territory in western Paraná. The vast artificial lake of Itaipu flooded dozens of tekoas, and several Guarani families were forced to leave their lands, some migrating to São Paulo. 

                At the same time, Indigenous leaders were persecuted by the State for their political activism in defense of their territories, or became targets of rural militias, as in the case of the great Guarani-Ñandeva leader Marçal de Souza. One of the pioneers of the Guarani struggles for the recovery and recognition of their ancestral territories, Marçal de Souza, Tupã-Y, was assassinated by hired gunmen in Mato Grosso do Sul in November 1983.

                Indigenous peoples’ resistance played a leading role in the fall of the military regime and the return to democracy in the 1980s.

                Under pressure from the Indigenous movement, the 1988 Constitution recognized the right of native peoples to their ancestral territories, rights that predate the very creation of the Brazilian State. The Constitution also guarantees the protection of Indigenous peoples’ social organization, customs, languages, beliefs, and traditions.

                In this context, like other territories and popular movements, Jaraguá underwent a growing process of political organization and rights advocacy. 

                Under pressure from the Indigenous movement, in 1984 — the year of the “Diretas Já” (Direct Elections Now) movement — FUNAI proceeded with the regularization of some Indigenous territories in the state of São Paulo, including a small part of the Jaraguá Indigenous Territory corresponding to Tekoa Ytu, demarcated in 1987. 

                Although these represented democratic advances, these demarcations were clearly insufficient, especially in the case of the Jaraguá Indigenous Territory, where the demarcated area covered only the 1.8-hectare perimeter of the Tekoa Ytu housing cluster. In this way, the demarcation ignored the territorial extent of the traditional use of the forests that sustains the _nhandereko_, the Guarani way of dwelling.

                With the return of democracy after twenty years of military dictatorship, Guarani communities began expanding their territorial recoveries in Jaraguá. In the 1990s, leaders Karaí Poty (José Fernandes) and Eunice, daughter of Indian Chief Jandira, founded Tekoa Pyau, called “new village.” In the following decades, several territorial recoveries took place: in 2005, Tekoa Itakupe; between 2016-2017, Tekoa Itaendy; in 2018, Tekoa Yvy Porã; and in 2023, the tekoas Pindó Mirim, Mirim, and Ka’aguy Mirim. 

                These territorial recoveries took place amid growing political organization among Guarani peoples at the national level. In 2006, the Comissão Guarani Yvyrupa (CGY) [Guarani Yvyrupa Commission] was created, an organization bringing together Guarani collectives from the South and Southeast regions of Brazil in the struggle for land. 

                The Jaraguá Indigenous Territory grew strong and increasingly populous. Through the CGY and its grassroots, Guarani leaders pushed for the demarcation of their territories.
